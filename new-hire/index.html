Permanent (direct hire)

Step 1

How we sell

The mindset behind everything else.

Every call and email has one goal: a meeting

You won’t close anything by phone or email. Use every touch to get a meeting, and build the relationship from there.

  1. Get the meetingThe only goal of every call and email.
  2. Run discoveryLearn their priorities, their team and who buys what.
  3. QualifyIs it a real opportunity? Who are the buyers? How much of your time does it earn?
  4. ExpandUse what you learn to meet more buyers and grow the account.
Warning: land a meeting, then let three months pass without them hearing from you, and you’re gone.

The four kinds of buyers

Several people shape every hire. Know which one you’re talking to.

Director / Sr Manager

Primary Buyer

Owns the problem and the hire. Usually your day-to-day contact, and your widest door.

Manager / Lead

Influencer

Shapes the decision without signing it. Win them and they sell for you.

VP / Head of

Key Decision Maker

Approves the spend and the vendor. Get to know them before a deal depends on them.

C-suite

Strategic Buyer

Buys larger services like projects or outsourcing, or oversees the teams that hire.

In the Top Accounts app, Buyer is the Primary Buyer and Sponsor is the Key Decision Maker or Strategic Buyer above them.

You don’t have to be the expert

Your job is the relationship. Be the client’s main point of contact: learn their pain points and what keeps managers up at night, then bring in the right experts: senior and lead recruiters, our consultants and candidates, the CIO Advisory practice and the NetSuite practice. Sharing useful content and posting on LinkedIn builds your credibility too.

Be honest. Never oversell.

If you don’t know, say so, then follow up. Trust wins more business than pitching.

“Let me get back to you on that.”
“Let me bring in my partner on this.”
If you oversell, you lose.

Be patient: some open in two months, some take two-plus years. Over time your list should shift toward the core buyers of project and contract services.

KARE accounts vs leads

KARE sorts accounts by what we’re trying to do there. Know your play before you reach out.

Protect what we have

Keep

Established clients. Stay close to the buyers, understand their priorities, keep the relationship strong, keep looking for growth.

Win something new

Attain

Companies we know use staffing firms but don’t work with us yet. Competitors are likely there. Break in and create revenue.

Win back an old client

Recapture

Clients who paid us before but have gone quiet, usually not in the past 18–24 months. Find out what happened and find a reason to restart.

Grow our foothold

Expand

We have some business there, with much more opportunity. Meet more buyers and sell more of what we do.

Not every company is a KARE account. Only current or past clients, or companies you already know use staffing firms. A net-new company is a target and goes through discovery first; once a meeting confirms they bring in outside help, it becomes an Attain account. KARE tells you the play; A or B tells you how much of your time it gets.

Accounts

Qualified companies worth pursuing, in any KARE play. Never give up on one: keep them in your cadence for good. Drop one only when there’s truly no relationship or no opportunity.

Leads

An opportunity worth pursuing: an open role, a competitor lead, hiring activity or another signal. Work it 6–8 weeks, then make it a target or move it to your email marketing list. Fallouts happen, so check back: “How’s it going?”

Depth beats breadth

A few accounts where you know several buyers well will do more for you than a long list you barely touch.

Start with 20 accounts

This is the plan our top producers follow. Skip the big list. Get really good at a few accounts first, then grow.

  1. Go deep on 20Pick 20 targets with the three questions below. Research each one, map the managers you can call on, and learn what’s changing, what they hire for and what tech they use.
  2. Find your flowSend the same short message every two weeks. Declare your on-site days and stack meetings so one visit fills a day. Most people reply between the 7th and 12th touch, so don’t stop early.
  3. Get the client visitThe goal of your first cycle. Discovery in person shows you how big the opportunity is, who buys and how to prioritize. You can’t rank accounts from your desk.
  4. Add targets as you goNew targets come from what you learn: referrals (ask who else to meet), leads, candidate intel, buyers who change jobs and new triggers.

A or B only after traction

Every company starts as a target. It earns an A or B once you’ve met a true buyer, ideally several. A gets most of your time; B gets a steady cadence. They move between A and B but never fall off your list.

Ready to add more targets when…

Your 20 are researched and mapped, your two-week rhythm is running, and you’ve landed client visits and asked who else to meet.

What makes a good target: three questions, in order

  1. Do they use agencies, or do we have a contract with them?The first filter. A company that already brings in outside help, or has a contract with us, can say yes. Check our placement history and contract list, your recruiters, job posts that mention contractors, and contractors on LinkedIn.
  2. How big is the department, and how many managers can you target?Rule of thumb: about one leader for every four people. A 20-person tech team has four or five managers plus a director or VP. More managers means more doors.
  3. Is something changing right now?A new leader, an acquisition, expansion, a new system, rapid hiring. Change creates work (Step 7).

Helpful extras: healthy financials, open roles in our practice areas, a warm way in, and being close to your other targets.

Only two reasons to walk

They’re locked behind a vendor management system with 30 vendors and no way in, or the account creates payment risk.

Otherwise, keep accounts

A 5-person company can grow to 100, and one account that looked unworkable ended up taking about 60 placements. Don’t write anyone off from your desk.

Weekly list review

Work A and B first, promote targets after a true-buyer meeting, add new targets, and keep notes and next-contact dates current.

Step 3

Plan your outreach

Your daily routine, how often to reach out, and how long to keep going.

What a good day looks like

  • Work 1–2 target companies a day. Go deep, then chase new leads.
  • Prep tomorrow today. Know who you’re calling and what’s new at each company.
  • Work every channel, and if something isn’t landing, change the angle, not the target.
  • Organize by priority: A accounts get most of your time, B accounts a steady cadence, targets until you meet a true buyer, and your entire list gets market intel so every buyer keeps seeing your name.

Much of this job is prep and follow-up. Check your list daily and review it every week.

Send the same core message to everyone relevant at the account. Change it only when there’s news, you have a specific candidate, or you’ll be on-site nearby. On a long thread, keep the thread but update the subject line. Once a decision-maker responds, spend less time on the other contacts there.

Existing relationship: “I know we haven’t connected in a while, but we worked together in the past. Why don’t we meet? How does next week look?”
New company: “We’re working with someone on the data side I think you’d want to see. Let me know if you’d like to talk to them.”

Add value on every touch (a check-in still beats going quiet): VMC lists matched to their stack, contractor and consultant bios, redeploys as consultants roll off, references and short case studies, timely market insight, salary data from the 2027 Salary Guide.

Consulting and project work almost never gets posted online. You find it through relationships that take six months to a year to build. Job ads mostly lead to permanent roles; discovery leads to project work.

Step 4

Prospecting: what to write and what to say

Short email, a real reason, a small ask and a specific day. Pick a template, swap in your words and anything in [brackets], and send it.

1

A reason

“We’ve worked with your company…”

2

One line of credibility

“I lead our Technology practice here…”

3

A small meeting

“Quick intro,” “stop by and say hello”

4

Easy timing

“How does Thursday or Friday look?”

Persist without writing a novel. One top producer got her first reply on the fourth email by sending the same short ask each time. For Technology: your practice = Technology practice; focus areas = software engineering, data and AI, infrastructure, cloud, security and enterprise applications; example role = D365 developer.

Email templates

What to say on a call

  1. Open the same way every time“Hey [Name], it’s [your name] from Vaco. Is this a bad time?” If “I’m busy”: “Totally get it, me too. I’ll be quick.” If “What’s this about?”: “Quick intro. I’ll give you the reason for the call.”
  2. Give your reasonWarm: “We’re already working with your [team], and I realized you and I haven’t connected yet, which honestly surprised me. I’d love a quick conversation on how we can support you too.” Growth signal: “I saw your team is expanding. I specialize in technology talent here in [city] and wanted to understand your team and initiatives for this year.” Never “I saw your job ad…”, and no specific opening unless you have a matched consultant.
  3. Ask for the meeting, in person first“Rather than cram this into a cold call, let’s grab 15 minutes. I’m in the area next week.” Warm account: “I’ll be out at your office Tuesday and Wednesday. Which days are you typically onsite?” Virtual is the fallback.
  4. If they rush you, reset calmly“Totally get it. I’ll keep this tight. We’re already supporting part of the business, and it made sense for us to connect too.”
  5. Close clean“Let’s grab 15 minutes. If it’s not useful, we’ll both know quickly.” / “Worst case, we meet once. Best case, we’re helpful down the road.”

When they push back

Don’t argue. Agree, reframe, and ask for the meeting again.

“We already have vendors.”

“Makes sense. Most of my clients did too before we met. This isn’t about replacing anyone. In the people business, not everything goes to plan, so I’d love to meet now so I already understand your team when something comes up.” You’re Plan B: another option when things get busy or niche.

“Send me something.”

“Happy to, but quick question first so I don’t send you noise…” Ask one light question. “That’s exactly why a quick conversation is easier. I can tell you in five minutes whether we’re even relevant.”

“Everything goes through talent acquisition or HR.”

“Understood. Happy to loop in HR when the time is right. This is more consulting-based work. Does HR typically manage consulting engagements?” If no: “Got it. I wasn’t calling about a role, more to understand your priorities so I’m aligned when something comes up.”

“I don’t have anything right now.”

“Totally fair. Most people don’t when we first connect. I just want you to know who I am when something does come up.”

“Not right now. Touch base after the holiday.”

“When does it make sense to connect? I’ll put something on the calendar for [month].”

“Why you over a big firm?”

Lead with you and your team: experience, long tenure, low turnover, one point of contact start to finish, real investment in their industry. Never trash a competitor; point to less red tape, more flexibility and a boutique feel with enterprise backing. Local: “I know how you hire, I’ve built relationships here, and I’m not going anywhere.” On price: “I’m not the cheapest. We pay our people well because we deliver. If we flex on price, help us out with an intro or a reference.”

Tone beats script. Learn the word tracks until they’re yours, then never read them. Disarming honesty works: “I’m not sure yet we’re the right fit. I don’t know enough about you yet.”

Send a VMC list: our top consultants, matched to your buyer

A VMC (Vaco marketable candidate) list shares a few of our top available consultants with a buyer, without waiting for an open role. The more targeted, the better it works.

Generic

Our five best people, sent to everyone. Easy to ignore.

Better

Matched to the buyer’s function and level, like data engineers for a Director of Data.

Best

Matched to this client’s tech stack, projects and the roles they keep hiring for.

  1. Learn your clients as you goRoles and levels they hire, contract vs perm, tech stack (Azure, Snowflake, NetSuite, D365…), current and coming projects, team size and gaps, who they’ve used before and why. Write it in your account notes every time.
  2. Pick 3–5 consultantsAvailable now or soon, vetted by your recruiters, matched to the client’s stack and level.
  3. One short line per personTitle, years, key tools, a recent project or industry, availability, contract or perm. Keep profiles blind: no last names or contact details.
  4. Send it with a reason and a small askTie it to something they told you, then ask for 15 minutes or an intro call with one of them.
  5. Follow up and learnNote who they liked and why. Your A and B accounts should see one regularly.
Step 5

Get in the room

Get on-site, run a great first meeting, and book the next one.

Meet in person whenever you can

Virtual is a good start, but on-site is where relationships get built. Group your targets by location so one booked meeting helps you book the ones around it. Being local shows you’re invested.

“I’ll be on-site next week meeting with a company near you. Let’s connect while I’m there.”

How to get on-site

We don’t follow up to get replies. We follow up to establish presence and persistence. Success is when they see you as someone who’s consistently around and relevant.

Declare on-site

Don’t ask permission. Tell them when you’ll be there.

Cadence beats cleverness

Same message every other week. Stay calm and don’t apologize. Silence isn’t failure.

Physical proof

Go on-site for other meetings even without a reply, then mention it.

One meeting changes everything

After the first one, doors open and introductions follow.

Avoid long explanations, apologizing for following up, and asking permission to exist. Memorize this: predictable presence beats perfect messaging. When it feels awkward: “That’s the part most people quit. We don’t.”

How to run your first meeting

Discovery is key. Ask, don’t pitch. Aim for them to talk 70% of the time: ask follow-ups (“No way. Tell me more.”), get comfortable with silence, and be a sounding board.

  1. Role and org chart“Tell me about your team. How many report to you? Full-time vs contractors?”
  2. Consultant history“When did you last bring in a consultant? What drove it?”
  3. What’s ahead“What’s coming this year where you’d want help?” Dig into anything technical: why it’s happening and when.
  4. Next steps“When should we connect next?” Ask why that timing.

More discovery questions when the conversation opens up: What’s driving this initiative, and why now? What resources do you have today, and where are the gaps? What happens if it isn’t addressed? What does success look like?

Before you leave: “I wouldn’t be doing my job if I didn’t ask: who else should I meet while I’m on site?” · “Is anything coming up we should keep an eye out for?”

Every meeting should end with a clearer org chart, known initiatives and a reason to talk again, plus what feeds your VMC list: roles, tech stack and upcoming projects.

Always book the next meeting

Never leave without it

“When should we reconnect?” Then calendar it.

Objection → date

“Not now” becomes “When does it make sense?” and an invite.

Send placeholders

“What if I send an invite for August?” Ask first, then send it.

Keep it small

Call it a “5-minute touch base.”

Meetings stack: book the next one every time and your calendar fills weeks out. Pick your on-site day, then call every nearby target: “I’m in your area next week. How does your schedule look?”

Step 7

Sales triggers 101

A trigger is something that just happened at a company that means they’ll soon want people or project help. It gives you a real reason to reach out, so you never have to ask “Are you hiring?”

They buy a competitor

Two teams, two sets of systems to merge → data help, project managers, integration consultants.

A new CIO starts

Priorities reset and the team reorganizes → fast talent moves, often in the first 90 days.

  1. Spot itNews, job boards and alerts.
  2. Check itReal and recent before you mention it.
  3. Use itReference it and ask for a short meeting.

Our sweet spots, work these first: buying or selling a company, getting their data in order, AI projects, and changing how finance works.

12 triggers to know first

Money moves

Raised money

New money gets spent on hiring, systems and expansion. Spot it: press releases, Crunchbase or PitchBook, a hiring spike.

Money moves

Buying or selling a company

Teams, systems and books have to be combined or split. Spot it: press releases, earnings calls, integration job posts.

People moves

Someone still has to do the work: interim cover, then rebuilding. Spot it: news, layoff notices.

New ERP or CRM

Project managers, data migration, testing and training. Spot it: job posts naming the system, vendor news.

Project, cloud, data, security and design help. Spot it: press, tech blogs, 2+ related job posts.

Privacy, cyber, banking, healthcare or AI rules on a deadline. Spot it: regulator news, compliance job posts.

Our technology focus

On the Northeast tech desk, weight triggers in this order: acquisitions and ownership changes > new technology leadership (and a new CFO) > system rollouts (ERP, cloud, data, AI) > cyber and compliance deadlines > other go-lives, funding and expansion. A cluster of new job posts in one tech area points to a project; scattered posts are just evidence.

Check it’s real, and where to look

Solid

Said officially: press release, public filing, announcement. Safe to mention.

Good

News or a signal backed by something else, like several job posts. Mention with care.

Just a lead

One alert, one job post or an AI summary. Verify first.

Freshness: most triggers 12 months · acquisitions 18 months · new leaders their first 90 days · job posts 6 months. Every trigger needs an exact date and a source you opened. Where to look: Sales Navigator alerts, Google Alerts on targets and executives, careers pages and job boards, news and business journals, public filings.

Every Monday the Northeast Tech Top Accounts app and the internal territory map are refreshed with new tech triggers, buyers, sponsors and new job openings. Start your week there.

How to use a trigger in outreach

Name what happened, say what it usually means, make a small ask. Then let discovery tell you the rest.

New leader: “Congrats on the new role. The first few months usually mean sizing up the team. I’d love to introduce myself so you know who to call. How does Thursday look?”
Acquisition: “Saw the news on the [Company] acquisition. Congrats. Integrations bring a lot of extra work on systems, data and reporting, and that’s where we help. Quick intro next week?”
Team growth: “I saw your team is expanding. I specialize in technology talent in [city] and wanted to understand your initiatives this year.”

Avoid: “I saw your job ad…”, pretending you know their problem, and three triggers in one email.

How we recruit, in 6 steps

Sales wins the job; you find the person. Great recruiting is speed plus honesty: the right people, fully closed, in front of the client fast.

Your specialty

Perm

Kush, Meggie, Tulsi

Direct-hire searches across our technology areas.

  1. Take a great intakeJoin the intake with the seller and hiring manager. Learn why the role is open, the must-have skills, pay range, location, start date and interview steps.
  2. Build your searchStart with people we already know: our candidates and consultants rolling off. Then LinkedIn, job boards and target companies. Go after the strongest realistic match first.
  3. Screen for fit and interestCheck skills, experience, motivation, pay expectations, availability and location. Ask why they’d move.
  4. Close before you submitEvery candidate is closed before their résumé goes out. See the next section.
  5. Present only the bestA short, accurate summary of why this person fits. Fewer, better candidates beat a pile of résumés.
  6. Manage it to the start, and stay closePrep for interviews, share feedback both ways, help close the offer, hand off to Lisa for the start sheet, then check in after they start.

Three rules

Speed wins

The first strong candidate in front of the client usually wins the job.

Always be honest

With candidates and clients. No surprises at offer time.

Always ask for leads

Every candidate knows managers who are hiring. Ask, and pass it to sales.

Your weekly numbers

A full week once you’re fully ramped. Aim for about half in month one, building to the full number over your first year. Your manager sets your exact targets.

Before you submit

Close every candidate

Most offer-stage surprises come from skipping this step. Confirm all of this before a résumé goes to a client.

If any answer is shaky, don’t submit yet. Fix it first, or move to the next candidate.

Questions that work on a screen

“What would make you leave where you are?”

“What’s most important in your next role?”

How we deliver

How a job gets filled

Sales wins the job; recruiting finds the person. Here’s the path from “we need someone” to their first day.

  1. Take the job orderTalk to the hiring manager: why the role is open, must-have skills, pay range, location, start date and interview steps.
  2. Recruiters search and screenThey find people and check skills, pay, availability and interest.
  3. We send the best candidatesOnly people who fit and truly want the job.
  4. Interviews and offerWe set up interviews, share feedback both ways, and help close the offer.
  5. StartOperations (Lisa) handles the contract, start sheet and onboarding.
  6. Stay closeCheck in with the client and the person. Happy clients lead to the next job.
